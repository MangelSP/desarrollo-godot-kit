name: game-designer-definition
topic: Role and definition of a game designer
confidence: consensus
sources: ["Think Like A Game Designer, George Rockwell, ch. 1 (pp. 30-33)"]
---
## Rules
- Define your job as crafting an *experience*, not just rules and components. The experience also depends on environment, other players, and each player's background assumptions.
- Treat three properties as the defining features of games: they have players, they have rules, and they are interactive. Use these to scope what you are building.
- Design for the interaction of players and rules as the mechanism that produces the experience.
- Identify your audience explicitly. Usually the players; sometimes viewers (game shows, sports, streams, e-sports). The same design principles apply, but the target of the experience differs.
- Assume players are the audience unless you deliberately design for spectators.

## Checklist
- [ ] Can you state in one sentence what experience the player should have?
- [ ] Have you listed the rules that drive that experience?
- [ ] Have you listed the interactions those rules create between players?
- [ ] Have you named the audience (players, viewers, or both)?
- [ ] Have you considered environment and player background as part of the experience, not just the game itself?

## Anti-patterns
- Treating the game as only rules plus components; ignoring the social and environmental context that produces the memorable experience.
- Designing for "everyone" without naming whether the audience is players or spectators.
- Assuming spectator design is a different discipline; the principles are the same, only the audience changes.

name: emotional-prediction-skill
topic: Empathy and predicting player emotion
confidence: opinion
sources: ["Think Like A Game Designer, George Rockwell, ch. 1 (pp. 30-33)"]
---
## Rules
- Judge design quality by your ability to predict two things: what actions players will take, and how those actions will make them feel.
- Accept that emotion is part of the job; the author frames game design as working in a "feeling factory". Do not skip the feelings step.
- Build empathy by first understanding your own emotional reactions to games; you cannot predict others' feelings without a reference model of your own.
- Expect this to be uncomfortable; treat discomfort as part of the skill, not a sign you are unsuited to design.

## Checklist
- [ ] For each key rule, can you predict the player action it invites?
- [ ] For each key action, can you predict the emotion it produces?
- [ ] Have you recorded your own emotional reactions while playing, as calibration data?
- [ ] Have you checked your prediction against real players rather than assuming it?

## Anti-patterns
- Dismissing emotion as "wishy-washy" and designing only mechanics.
- Assuming players feel what you intended without testing.
- Relying only on abstract theory instead of your own felt experience of games.

name: three-precursor-habits
topic: Habits that develop design intuition
confidence: opinion
sources: ["Think Like A Game Designer, George Rockwell, ch. 1 (pp. 30-33)"]
---
## Rules
- Develop three habits in parallel: play lots of games, watch people play lots of games, and bring awareness to the emotions that arise during play.
- Treat observation of players as a continuous input, not a one-time test. Collect both explicit feedback (what they say) and implicit feedback (what they do and feel).
- Use these habits to build intuition; intuition is the mechanism that turns initial concepts into great games.
- Play bad games too, not only good ones; failures are as informative as successes.

## Checklist
- [ ] Are you playing regularly, including games outside your genre?
- [ ] Are you watching others play, in person or on streams?
- [ ] Are you noting emotions during play, for yourself and others?
- [ ] Are you capturing both explicit and implicit player feedback?
- [ ] Are you feeding that feedback back into your designs?

## Anti-patterns
- Designing in isolation without watching real players.
- Collecting only verbal feedback and ignoring behavior and emotional signals.
- Playing only your favorite genre, which narrows your reference set.

name: start-with-board-and-card-games
topic: Low-cost iteration via traditional games
confidence: opinion
sources: ["Think Like A Game Designer, George Rockwell, ch. 1 (pp. 30-33)"]
---
## Rules
- Start learning design with traditional board and card games regardless of the type of game you eventually want to make.
- Choose this starting point because iteration cost is very low: try an idea, test it, get feedback, repeat with little overhead.
- Use the fast loop to learn what works and what doesn't, which is what actually improves design skill.
- Begin analysis with your favorite games: identify what attracts you to them.
- Watch for moments of intense emotional response (good or bad) in yourself and others; note what led to them, then plan how to evoke or avoid those causes in your own designs.

## Checklist
- [ ] Have you picked a small paper or card prototype to iterate on this week?
- [ ] Can you run a full try-test-feedback cycle with minimal setup cost?
- [ ] Have you identified what specifically attracts you to your favorite games?
- [ ] Have you logged at least one intense emotional moment and its cause?
- [ ] Have you translated that cause into a design intent for your own game?

## Anti-patterns
- Starting with a high-production-value digital prototype where each iteration is expensive.
- Studying only games you love and skipping analysis of why they work.
- Noting that a moment was fun or frustrating without tracing the rule or situation that caused it.

name: core-design-loop
topic: The six-step core design loop
confidence: consensus
sources: ["Think Like A Game Designer, George Rockwell, ch. 1 (pp. 30-33)"]
---
## Rules
- Run every project through the six-step core design loop: Inspiring, Framing, Brainstorming, Prototyping, Testing, Iterating.
- Inspiring: decide what type of experience you will create.
- Framing: assess your parameters and deadlines before generating ideas.
- Brainstorming: get ideas down on paper.
- Prototyping: bring your best ideas to life.
- Testing: learn what works and what doesn't.
- Iterating: use what you learned to improve the next cycle.
- Optimize for cycle speed and iteration count: the faster you move through the loop and the more iterations you complete, the better the game. Treat iteration count as a primary quality lever.

## Checklist
- [ ] Experience type defined before any idea generation?
- [ ] Constraints and deadlines written down before brainstorming?
- [ ] Ideas captured on paper, not held in your head?
- [ ] A playable prototype exists for the best ideas?
- [ ] A test has produced concrete "works / doesn't work" findings?
- [ ] Findings fed back into the next cycle?
- [ ] Cycle time measured, so you can shorten it next round?

## Anti-patterns
- Skipping Framing and brainstorming without knowing your constraints.
- Prototyping everything instead of the best ideas.
- Testing without recording what worked and what didn't.
- Treating the loop as one-pass; stopping after the first iteration.
- Long cycles with few iterations, which the author treats as directly worse output.

name: overcoming-fear-ego-uncertainty
topic: psychology of getting started
confidence: opinion
sources: ["Think Like A Game Designer, George Rockwell, ch. 2 (pp. 34-41)"]
---
## Rules
- Treat fear as the default blocker for creative work, not a personal defect. Fear has two components: ego (identity fused to ideas) and uncertainty (unknown outcome). Name which one is blocking you before acting. WHY: naming the specific fear makes it addressable instead of a vague reason to procrastinate.
- Decouple self-worth from idea quality. Evaluate your own idea as if a stranger proposed it. WHY: if "bad idea" equals "bad person", you will hide ideas from testing and never learn what works.
- Judge designers by idea throughput, not hit rate: 20 bad ideas + 2 good ones beats 0 ideas. WHY: bad ideas are the raw material that seeds good ones; volume is the trainable skill.
- Accept that your first game will be bad. Plan for iteration, not for a perfect first release. WHY: expecting perfection on attempt one creates paralysis; the try-fail-learn loop is the actual design process.
- Adopt "done is better than perfect" as the release criterion for prototypes. WHY: an unfinished perfect design teaches you nothing; a finished flawed one teaches you everything.
## Checklist
- [ ] Identify whether the current block is ego-driven (fear of judgment) or uncertainty-driven (fear of the unknown).
- [ ] Re-read your idea as if someone else wrote it, then decide if it is worth testing.
- [ ] Confirm the current task is a testable step, not a perfection pass.
## Anti-patterns
- Keeping ideas private "until they are good" — guarantees zero feedback and zero improvement.
- Waiting for the "right time" to start; it never arrives and the delay compounds into shame and further delay.
- Treating a failed prototype as a verdict on your ability rather than data about the design.

name: start-small-next-action
topic: breaking down projects
confidence: consensus
sources: ["Think Like A Game Designer, George Rockwell, ch. 2 (pp. 34-41)"]
---
## Rules
- Never plan the whole project before starting. Ask only: "What is the next physical action that moves this forward?" WHY: a full game is amorphous and intimidating; a single next step is not.
- Make the next action concrete and physical (look up a tutor, call a friend, buy supplies) rather than mental ("think about design"). WHY: physical actions are unambiguous and completable, so they generate momentum.
- Commit to completing that one step within 48 hours. WHY: a short, near deadline converts intention into action before motivation decays.
- After completing a step, immediately define and commit to the next small step. WHY: chained small steps accumulate into large projects without ever facing the whole mountain.
- Design your own workflow as a sequence of small actionable tasks, one at a time, without fixating on the end goal. WHY: step-by-step progress is what actually ships games.
## Checklist
- [ ] Is the next action physical and doable in one sitting?
- [ ] Is it scheduled within 48 hours?
- [ ] Have you defined the following step before stopping?
## Anti-patterns
- Scoping the first task as "design the game" — too large to start, so it never starts.
- Answering every open question (market fit, feasibility, production) before taking step one.
- Waiting for motivation instead of using a near-term commitment to create it.

name: deadlines-force-completion
topic: productivity and shipping
confidence: opinion
sources: ["Think Like A Game Designer, George Rockwell, ch. 2 (pp. 34-41)"]
---
## Rules
- Attach a deadline to every design goal. Set it reasonable but aggressive, then stick to it. WHY: deadlines force focus on the essential and convert open-ended work into committed work.
- Make deadlines public or socially enforced (schedule a playtest night in two weeks, post a chapter weekly). WHY: external accountability adds pressure that self-imposed deadlines lack.
- Use external deadlines as a substitute for experience: even with no idea what you are doing, a hard deadline produces finished work. WHY: completion under constraint teaches more than unlimited tinkering.
- Write down 3-5 goals with explicit deadlines, each completable within one year. WHY: a bounded, dated list turns vague ambition into a plan.
- Post the commitment publicly (forum, social media) to make it real. WHY: public commitment raises the cost of quitting.
## Checklist
- [ ] Every listed goal has a date next to it.
- [ ] Each goal is achievable within 12 months.
- [ ] At least one deadline is witnessed by other people.
- [ ] A concrete consequence or event (playtest, post) is tied to the deadline.
## Anti-patterns
- Treating a game as a "side project" with no date — it loses to every more pressing demand.
- Setting deadlines so loose they create no focus, or so tight they guarantee failure and demoralization.
- Reading productivity advice and finishing the book without committing to any dated action.

## Rules
- Generate 10 game ideas in under 10 minutes without judging quality. WHY: the act of writing bad ideas down lowers the barrier to producing good ones.

- Editing or discarding ideas mid-exercise instead of capturing raw output.

## Rules
- Before starting a game project, write down your personal reason for making it (self-expression, career change, proving skill). Keep it visible where you work.
- Make the "why" visceral, not abstract: a concrete image or phrase you feel, not a vague mission statement.
- When motivation drops, re-read the why before deciding to quit or pause.
- WHY: A visible, emotional reminder of purpose is what carries you through the long stretch where progress feels like two steps back for every step forward.

## Checklist
- [ ] Written "why" exists in one or two sentences.
- [ ] It is placed somewhere you see daily (desk, monitor, notebook).
- [ ] It names a feeling or outcome, not a task.

## Anti-patterns
- Relying on willpower alone with no written reminder.
- Copying someone else's reason for making games instead of your own.
- Treating the "why" as a one-time exercise and never revisiting it.

## Rules
- Split goals into two lists: immediate/short-term goals and long-term goals.

- Storing all goals only in an app you open rarely.
- Setting so many short-term goals that none get finished.

name: core-design-loop
topic: game-design-process
confidence: consensus
sources: ["Think Like A Game Designer, George Rockwell, ch. 4 (pp. 47-48)"]
---
## Rules
- Structure all game design work as a six-step loop: Inspiring → Framing → Brainstorming → Prototyping → Testing → Iterating, then back to the start.
- Treat the loop as cyclic, not linear: after Iterating you re-enter Inspiring/Framing with what you learned, and repeat until the product is finished.
- Optimize for loop speed, not for perfection at any single step. The core skill is completing iterations as quickly and efficiently as possible. WHY: each pass produces real information (from Testing) that no amount of upfront planning can substitute for.
- On your first project, run every step fully and in order. WHY: you need to feel each phase before you can judge which steps to compress.
- Only after several complete loops may you take shortcuts or modify the sequence to fit your working style. WHY: shortcuts are only safe once you know what each step contributes.
- Budget your effort so that Prototyping and Testing happen early and often; they are the steps that generate evidence.
- Keep the loop running even when a step feels unproductive — the value is in the cycle, not in any single phase.

## Checklist
- [ ] Can I name which of the six steps I am currently in?
- [ ] Have I completed at least one full loop (all six steps) before customizing the process?
- [ ] Is my current loop shorter in time than my previous one?
- [ ] Does every loop end with a concrete change carried into the next Iterating pass?
- [ ] Am I spending disproportionate time in one step (usually Brainstorming) instead of moving to Prototyping?

## Anti-patterns
- Treating the six steps as a one-way waterfall that ends at "finished product" — it is a loop.
- Polishing a single step (e.g. brainstorming hundreds of ideas) before ever prototyping. WHY: untested ideas are unverified assumptions.
- Adopting shortcuts or skipping steps on your first project, before you have experienced the full loop.
- Measuring progress by how much design documentation exists rather than by how many loops have been completed.

name: idea-generation-abundance-mindset
topic: creativity and idea generation
confidence: consensus
sources: ["Think Like A Game Designer, George Rockwell, ch. 5 (pp. 49-56)"]
---
## Rules
- Treat ideas as cheap and execution as the bottleneck: assume a 10/90 split between idea value and execution value. WHY: a mediocre idea executed well can succeed, while a brilliant idea executed poorly rarely does.
- Capture every idea immediately in a journal or notes app; review the journal regularly. WHY: most good ideas are lost because they are never written down.
- Share ideas early and often with other designers; adopt an abundance mentality. WHY: iterative design requires constant testing and feedback, and you will always generate more ideas.
- Borrow liberally from existing works and combine two or more concepts into a new experience. WHY: all creation is recombination; nothing is fully original.
- Let personal interests and passions guide which concepts you pursue. WHY: authentic voice emerges naturally when you follow genuine curiosity.
- Do not treat inspiration as a rare event; treat it as a trainable habit. WHY: inspiration is the easiest and least important part of design, so it should not block progress.

## Checklist
- [ ] Keep a running idea journal and add to it daily.
- [ ] Review the journal at least weekly and flag ideas worth developing.
- [ ] Share at least one concept with another person before building a prototype.
- [ ] Identify two existing games or experiences you can combine for each new concept.
- [ ] Pick one concept and start prototyping rather than waiting for a "perfect" idea.

## Anti-patterns
- Hoarding ideas out of fear of theft; this prevents feedback and kills the project.
- Waiting for a "next big thing" idea before starting work.
- Cherishing an idea so much that it cannot be scrutinized or changed.
- Treating creativity as innate genius rather than a repeatable process.

## Rules
- Run the ideation exercise in six sequential 20-minute chunks; they can be split across sessions but must stay in order. WHY: each step builds on the previous list.
- Step 1: list favorite games and genres, including childhood games and non-video categories (board, RPG, drinking games).

- [ ] 20 min: gems list with highlights.
- [ ] 20 min: crud list, including subtractive ideas.
- [ ] 20 min: pattern scan and rapid concept writing.
- [ ] 20 min: select one concept and begin prototyping.

## Anti-patterns
- Censoring or editing ideas during the rapid-writing step.
- Only adding features to favorite games instead of removing obstacles.
- Skipping the "games you hate" step and losing its lessons.
- Ending the exercise without committing to one concept to prototype.

name: framing-constraints-breed-creativity
topic: Game design framing and constraints
confidence: consensus
sources: ["Think Like A Game Designer, George Rockwell, ch. 6 (pp. 57-61)"]
---
## Rules
- Treat framing as the first step of every design cycle: define the space you will work in before generating ideas. WHY: unlimited possibility is intimidating and slows decisions.
- Deliberately hold some variables constant while you change others. WHY: game systems are interconnected, so changing one part ripples through the whole design; fixed variables make each change measurable.
- Write down your frame (audience, hook, restrictions, deadline) and keep it visible through the first design cycle. WHY: written parameters guide decisions and prevent scope drift.
- Treat the frame as revisable, not permanent: you may relax it after the first cycle, but wrestle with the limits first. WHY: constraints force focus and speed up the move to prototyping.
- When designing solo, act as your own client and set the frame yourself (audience, theme, deadline). WHY: hired designers get framing for free; self-directed designers must manufacture it or drift.
- Set an artificial deadline even with no external pressure. WHY: deadlines force focus on what matters and stop endless tweaking and procrastination.
- Keep the frame small at the start; broaden later if needed. WHY: a narrow frame is easier to design against and easier to validate.

## Checklist
- [ ] Target audience defined (age, hobbies, interests, current games played, motivations, purchases).
- [ ] A single mental picture of one representative player written down.
- [ ] Hook written in one or two sentences max.
- [ ] External restrictions listed (deadline, IP/brand, cost, platform, language).
- [ ] Artificial restrictions added if none exist naturally.
- [ ] Deadline committed for a playable prototype.
- [ ] Frame stored where you will re-read it during the cycle.

## Anti-patterns
- Designing for "everyone" — a game for everyone is a game for no one.
- Starting from a blank page with zero limitations.
- Changing many variables at once, making it impossible to tell what caused a design change.
- Treating self-imposed parameters as unbreakable law (they are guidance, not stone).
- Skipping the written frame and keeping it "in your head".
- Refining and tweaking indefinitely instead of shipping a prototype.

## Rules
- Pick one narrow target audience for the design cycle, not a broad market. WHY: a broad target dilutes every design decision.
- Describe the audience by concrete attributes: age range, hobbies, interests, what they buy, what games they play now, and why they play them. WHY: these answers convert a vague demographic into design guidance.
- Envision one specific person in that group and design for that individual. WHY: a single concrete player is easier to satisfy than an abstract segment.
- Default to designing for yourself if no other audience is required. WHY: you can instantly tell when the game succeeds or fails for you.
- Even when designing for yourself, recruit playtesters who also match the target audience. WHY: designers are blind to their own shortcomings; outside testers supply the reality check.

## Checklist
- [ ] Age range written down.
- [ ] Hobbies and interests listed.
- [ ] What they buy listed.
- [ ] Games they currently play listed.
- [ ] Reasons they play those games listed.
- [ ] One representative player pictured in detail.
- [ ] At least one playtester outside the design team who fits the target audience identified.

## Anti-patterns
- Targeting "gamers" as a whole.
- Assuming your own taste represents the audience when you are not the target.
- Playtesting only with the design team.
- Leaving the audience as an unwritten assumption.

name: framing-hook-elevator-pitch
topic: Game hook and elevator pitch
confidence: consensus
sources: ["Think Like A Game Designer, George Rockwell, ch. 6 (pp. 57-61)"]
---
## Rules
- Every game needs an explicit hook that differentiates it from the hundreds of games released each year. WHY: storefronts and ads give you only seconds to earn attention.
- Express the hook in one or two sentences maximum. WHY: if it takes longer, the hook is not yet clear enough to communicate.
- Use the elevator-pitch test: imagine one minute with a wealthy investor and describe why the game is worth their time. WHY: it forces the shortest convincing version of the idea.
- A reliable hook pattern is combining two known exciting things in a new way (e.g., familiar mechanic + unexpected theme, or two genres merged). WHY: novelty built on recognition is fast to grasp.
- Write the hook down explicitly rather than leaving it implicit in the design. WHY: it becomes a filter for later feature decisions.
- The pitch describes only the unique hook, not the whole game. WHY: the goal is to spark interest, not to explain all systems.

## Checklist
- [ ] Hook written in ≤2 sentences.
- [ ] Hook names what makes the game different from existing titles.
- [ ] Hook can be delivered verbally in under a minute.
- [ ] Hook traces back to the inspiration from the earlier design phase.
- [ ] Hook stored with the frame and re-checked when adding features.

## Anti-patterns
- Describing the full ruleset instead of the hook.
- Hooks that require several sentences or a paragraph to land.
- Copying an existing game's pitch without a differentiating twist.

name: three-phase-brainstorming
topic: ideation process for game design
confidence: consensus
sources: ["Think Like A Game Designer, George Rockwell, ch. 7 (pp. 62-70)"]

## Rules
- Run brainstorming in three strictly ordered phases: Creation → Organization → Elimination. Each phase uses a different mode of thinking, so mixing them degrades output.
- Allocate at least 20 minutes per phase. Do not shorten phases even if you think you already have "the answer" — ramp-up time is required to reach peak idea generation.
- Phase 1 (Creation): write ideas continuously; if you stop writing for more than ~20 seconds you are self-censoring. Aim for 3–4 alternatives per sub-problem before moving on, to get past the obvious solution.
- Phase 2 (Organization): cluster ideas into categories (core mechanics, setup, theme, resources, components, turn structure, victory conditions, level concepts, story ideas). Grouping triggers new gap-filling ideas — add them as they appear.
- Phase 3 (Elimination): pick exactly one core idea to prototype. Goal is to prototype as few things as possible, not as many.
- In Phase 3, ask "what is the best part of this design?" and find the cheapest, fastest way to test it. List the minimum components needed; if the list is long, refine the idea further.
- Accept leaving good ideas unused — they can be revisited in the next design cycle.
- WHY: separating divergent and convergent thinking prevents premature criticism from killing ideas, and a single focused prototype keeps iteration cheap.

## Checklist
- [ ] Core inspiration and initial frame/parameters defined before starting.
- [ ] Phase 1 timed (≥20 min), output is a large messy list.
- [ ] Phase 2 timed (≥20 min), output is a mind map with labeled clusters.
- [ ] Phase 3 timed (≥20 min), output is one core idea + minimum component list.
- [ ] Mind map drawn on a large surface (big sheet, whiteboard, or digital tool like FreeMind/WorkFlowy).
- [ ] During Phase 2, asked: How do you get from here to there? What other components must exist? How would a player experience this?

## Anti-patterns
- Jumping to elimination early (common for analytical personalities) — kills idea volume.
- Judging or editing ideas during Creation ("that will never work", "that's stupid").
- Skipping exercises to "read the book first" as disguised procrastination.
- Cramped paper or crowded workspace — visual clutter cues the brain to stop generating ideas.
- Spending less than 20 minutes on any phase.

## Rules
- Every prototype must contain exactly three artifacts: written rules, physical/digital components, and a list of questions the test should answer. Missing any one of them turns a test into a demo.

- Define the test questions before the session, tailored to the core concept under test. A formal questionnaire is not required. WHY: unfocused feedback produces opinions instead of data.
- Treat each prototype as a scientific experiment: state the hypothesis it tests. WHY: it forces you to decide in advance what result would falsify the design.
- Expect the first prototype to be bad and plan to revise it, not perfect it. WHY: perfectionism on prototype #1 is the most common reason games never ship.
- Do not judge prototype quality against the polished commercial games that inspired you. WHY: that comparison kills motivation before any learning happens.
- Aim to learn cheaply from failed assumptions rather than to be validated. WHY: a rejected idea discovered in one day is a win, not a loss.

- [ ] Components cover all information the player must track

- Prototyping without a defined core concept — you end up testing nothing specific.
- Skipping the written rules because "it's obvious" — iteration history is lost.

## Rules

- Match the audience to the prototype's polish level. Other game designers are the ideal early testers because they see past clunkiness to the mechanics. WHY: non-designers get distracted by missing art and unbalanced numbers.

- New designers should start with games they and their friends already enjoy. WHY: the audience is easy to find and broken mechanics are obvious to people who know the genre.
- For other demographics (e.g. small children), actively recruit that audience; do not substitute your own group. WHY: feedback from the wrong demographic is misleading.
- Use flexible tools first (cards, tiles, markers, sticky notes) and only invest in harder-to-change prototypes as confidence in the core design grows. WHY: low edit cost removes resistance to iterating.
- Widen the audience as the prototype hardens: rough paper tests with designers first, broader and more polished tests later.

- [ ] Can this test be run with materials I already own?
- [ ] Is the session scoped to the core mechanic only?
- [ ] Are testers able to look past missing polish?

- [ ] Is the audience representative of the target player?

- Recoding a digital level to test a rule change that a paper test could answer.
- Showing an early prototype to a general audience and drawing conclusions from their reaction to the art.
- Locking in expensive components or code before the core loop is validated.
- Running long sessions instead of many short iterations.

name: iterating-on-playtest-feedback

confidence: consensus

---
## Rules
- After every playtest, classify the result into exactly one of three buckets before doing anything else: (1) core concept works, (2) core concept doesn't work, (3) mixed/unclear. WHY: the bucket determines which iteration procedure you run; skipping classification leads to random changes.
- Treat "players ask to play again" as the strongest single signal that the core concept works. WHY: unprompted replay requests are behavioral evidence, not politeness.
- If the concept works, do not immediately scale up. Re-test with at least one different playgroup first. WHY: a single group (friends, family) can be biased in your favor.
- If the concept works, keep iterating on the same core mechanic with sharper questions rather than adding new systems. WHY: the goal of each loop is to make the proven core shine, not to dilute it.
- If the concept fails, treat it as useful data, not a verdict on you. Extract lessons and feed them into the next prototype. WHY: concrete negative feedback tells you what to change; it is the fastest information you can get.
- If feedback is mixed, pick one of two explicit paths: "try again" (new playgroup, more refined prototype, or a small tweak to the core) or "let it sit" (park the design and work on something else). WHY: mixed results give no clear next step, and undirected tweaking is how designs get orphaned.
- Before parking any design, write down rules, parameters, core concept, and brainstorm ideas in an organized place. WHY: designers forget details fast once the design leaves working memory; undocumented designs are effectively lost.
- When parking a design, set a calendar reminder to return in a few weeks. WHY: fresh eyes reveal the big picture that was invisible mid-project.
- Keep cycling the core design loop (inspiration → framing → brainstorm → prototype → test) until the core mechanic genuinely shines. WHY: each pass sharpens the questions you ask, which is the actual skill being built.
- If the concept works, progressively move into later design phases to refine the product; if something breaks, circle back and iterate. WHY: refinement and iteration are alternating modes, not a one-way pipeline.

## Checklist
- Did I record the playtest result as works / doesn't work / mixed?
- Did I keep ego out and read the data honestly?
- Did players ask to play again? (strongest success signal)
- If success: have I scheduled a second, different playgroup?

- If failure: did I answer "why didn't it work?", "what unforeseen thing can be changed or removed?", "what did people enjoy more than expected?"
- If mixed: did I explicitly choose try-again or let-it-sit?
- If parking: are all rules, parameters, core concept, and brainstorm notes written down and organized?
- If parking: is a return reminder set for a few weeks out?
- Did I re-scan the inspiration list and brainstorm list for the next core concept to test?

- Declaring victory from one enthusiastic playtest group (especially friends/family) and jumping straight into production.
- Treating a failed prototype as personal failure and abandoning the design loop.
- Reacting to mixed feedback by making random small changes with no hypothesis.
- Shelving a design without documenting it, then losing it permanently.
- Leaving a parked design with no calendar reminder to return.
- Adding new features to a working core instead of refining the core itself.

name: resource-types
topic: Engine Design — Resources
confidence: consensus
sources: ["Think Like A Game Designer, George Rockwell, ch. 12 (pp. 98-106)"]
---
## Rules
- Enumerate every resource the player can spend or hold (cards, gold, action points, time, units). WHY: tension comes from the interaction between limited resources; undefined resources can't be balanced.
- Classify each resource as scaling, fixed, or accumulating:
  - Scaling: starts small, grows each turn/interval regardless of use (lands per turn, energy per turn, RPG levels). Gives a sense of progression; small early effects stay relevant, big effects arrive late; supports early-vs-late strategy tension.
  - Fixed: constant value, must be spent this turn or lost (one action per turn, one move per piece, one command per unit). Simple and easy to learn; the default building block — but you must add progression by other means.
  - Accumulating: carries over unspent (cards in hand, elixir, gold). Reduces per-turn spending pressure but increases decision complexity because spending must be evaluated against all future turns.
- Cap accumulating resources (max hand size, mana cap) to control complexity. WHY: the smaller the cap relative to per-turn income, the closer the resource behaves to a fixed resource.
- Choose resource types deliberately for the feel and learning curve you want. WHY: resource choice strongly affects evoked feelings and rules complexity.

## Checklist
- [ ] Full resource list written down.
- [ ] Each resource labeled scaling / fixed / accumulating.
- [ ] For accumulating resources: a cap defined and justified.
- [ ] For fixed-only games: a separate progression mechanism identified.
- [ ] Interactions between resources described (which tension each pair creates).

## Anti-patterns
- Adding accumulating resources with no cap, producing analysis paralysis.
- Using only fixed resources and expecting a sense of progression.
- Introducing a new resource for every new mechanic.

name: interaction-axes
topic: Engine Design — Axes of Interaction
confidence: consensus
sources: ["Think Like A Game Designer, George Rockwell, ch. 12 (pp. 98-106)"]
---
## Rules
- Define how players interact — with the system (single-player) or with each other. WHY: interaction is what separates games from other media and is the biggest driver of the engine's feel.
- Pick from three interaction families:
  - Shared resources: a limited pool multiple players draw from. Subdivide into hot (visible theft/claiming, e.g., map territory, center row) and cold (hidden drafting, passing packs). Hot = more tension and excitement but more anxiety and hostility; cold = same abstract mechanics, softer emotional charge.
  - Direct attack: players attack or destroy each other's resources (life totals, pieces, units). Feels deeply interactive; each gain is the opponent's loss.
  - Deduction: players read other players (social) or read clues in the game (puzzle). Social deduction works with light rules if information is revealed in bits over time; puzzle deduction lives on tension-and-release and must be tuned to the player's difficulty level.
- When using direct attack, avoid letting players attack resources that are critical to playing the game. WHY: attacking critical resources creates a runaway-leader effect; attacking non-critical ones (life totals) does not.
- For puzzle deduction, provide difficulty modes and/or unlockable hints. WHY: too hard = frustration only; too easy = no satisfaction.
- For social deduction, structure the game so each player leaks partial information over time. WHY: gaps in information are what force deduction.

## Checklist
- [ ] Every interaction axis in the game listed and classified.
- [ ] Shared resources marked hot or cold.
- [ ] Direct-attack targets checked: is any of them required to keep playing?
- [ ] Deduction games: information-reveal schedule defined.
- [ ] Puzzle games: difficulty options or hint system planned.

## Anti-patterns
- Attacking a resource the victim needs to keep participating (creates runaway leader and feel-bad).
- Mixing hot shared resources into a game meant to be low-conflict.
- Puzzle games with a single fixed difficulty.
- Social deduction with no drip-feed of information.

confidence: consensus

## Rules
- Every game needs a defined ending mechanism, even if it has no winner. WHY: without an end condition the play session has no shape and no payoff.

- [ ] Time-limit games: decide whether the timer is explicit or implicit.
- [ ] Goal-achieving games: decide whether progress is public or hidden.
- [ ] Ripple effects of the end condition on other systems reviewed.

## Anti-patterns
- Multiplayer games where players can be knocked out in the first minutes.
- Time-limit games with a fully predictable end moment (kills the tension).
- Goal-achieving games with fully public progress in a runaway-leader scenario.
- Shipping a game with no defined end condition.

name: engine-development-scope
topic: Core engine development phase
confidence: consensus
sources: ["Think Like A Game Designer, George Rockwell, ch. 13 (pp. 107-111)"]
---
## Rules
- Treat engine development as the framing phase: at its end the game must be **fun and meet design goals**, but it does **not** need to be balanced or polished. WHY: balance and polish are cheap to fix later; engine-level decisions have long-reaching impact and are expensive to change.
- Prototype only the minimum component set needed to test the engine, not a complete game. WHY: a 5-minute focused session teaches more than a 45-minute full playthrough, and lets you iterate more per unit of time.
- Concrete starting quantities: fighting game → 3–4 characters with defined traits; trading card game → 2–3 pre-built decks. WHY: a small number of permutations is enough to expose the available strategies.
- Keep components "nuts and bolts" — mostly plain effects, with only a few boundary-pushing ones. WHY: too many exotic components obscure what is happening at the engine level.
- Set ballpark numbers (component count, value ranges, target game length) even if they are wrong. WHY: you need a working range to test your assumptions against.
- By the end of the phase you must be able to write a complete rule set (movement, draw rate, line-of-sight, stun, resource conversion), knowing it will change. WHY: a full rule set is what reveals how components interact.
- Iterate across several *complete* candidate rule sets rather than patching one. WHY: only complete sets show whether the game is fun and not broken.
- Write everything down — rules, numbers, rejected ideas. WHY: prevents forgetting prior work, enables other designers/playtesters to test from text, and becomes source material for later design writing.
- Keep rejected rules in an archive. WHY: collectible/expansion design is largely about breaking engine rules, and you can only break rules you remember (e.g. a "doesn't attack on opponent's turn" card is only designable if you know the default is attacking on both turns).
- Move numbers in big chunks: when in doubt, **double or halve**. WHY: large shifts tell you the direction faster than many small tweaks; fine-tuning comes later.
- If a mechanic feels too weak or too dominant, double or halve its numbers before anything else. WHY: it quickly reveals whether the mechanic matters at all.
- Watch what players actually do and check whether the core loop rewards it. WHY: systemic avoidance of interaction signals a broken core loop.
- Make natural player instincts the correct play most of the time; if players consistently choose an action, embrace it rather than punish it. WHY: fighting player instinct creates friction and hides the real design problem.
- Beware your own play style diverging from the average player's. WHY: the author's personal "improve deck first" instinct hid that casual players attacked monsters first, delaying a needed rebalance.
- Judge the phase by player experience: does it evoke the intended feelings, is the length close to target, is the vision still intact? WHY: player experience is the only metric that matters.
- Leave the phase only when the fundamentals feel stable; fix feel problems now. WHY: component design can patch many issues, but engine decisions persist for the game's life.

## Checklist
- [ ] Minimum viable component set exists (e.g. 3–4 characters / 2–3 decks).
- [ ] Ballpark numbers chosen: component count, value ranges, target session length.
- [ ] A complete written rule set exists for the current iteration.
- [ ] At least two alternative complete rule sets have been tried.
- [ ] Rejected rules and their reasons are recorded somewhere retrievable.
- [ ] Numbers have been stress-tested with at least one doubling/halving pass.
- [ ] Observed player behavior matches the intended core loop incentives.
- [ ] Average-player behavior compared against your own instincts.
- [ ] Game is fun and hits design goals (balance/polish explicitly deferred).

## Anti-patterns
- Building a full game before testing the engine.
- Adding many exotic components early, which masks engine-level problems.
- Chasing exact numbers during this phase instead of ballparks.
- Making many small numeric tweaks instead of large jumps.
- Keeping rules only in your head or in code.
- Throwing away rejected rules instead of archiving them.
- Balancing around your own preferred play style rather than observed player behavior.
- Polishing or balancing before the fundamentals feel right.

## Rules
- Before designing individual components, define top-level categories (e.g. colors, classes, roles, weapon types, property types). WHY: categories give every component a "home" and reveal gaps that suggest what to design next.
- Define each category by answering 3 mechanical questions: what it does mechanically, what it is best at, what it is worst at. WHY: explicit strengths/weaknesses make it obvious which components belong and which do not.
- Define each category by answering 3 thematic questions: what it represents, what it wants most, what it hates or fears most. WHY: theme plus mechanics together let both designers and players predict what fits.
- Aim for the smallest set of questions that still separates your categories; add more questions only as game complexity grows. WHY: over-specifying early slows you down and locks in decisions you have not tested.
- Treat categories as a hierarchy: after top level, repeat the same process to create subcategories/subtypes inside each category. WHY: nested structure handles hundreds of components without losing coherence.
- Design cycles: hold most variables constant and vary one trait across categories (e.g. same cost, same magnitude, different effect per category). WHY: constant elements make differences pop, teaching players what each category is about.
- Also build cycles inside a single category (small / medium / large version of a core effect). WHY: internal cycles highlight a category's signature feature.
- When unsure, add more cycles and more structure rather than fewer. WHY: structure is cheap to add and hard to overdo; it speeds both design and player learning.
- Break structure only deliberately, to spotlight a specific component, and only after the structure is well established. WHY: a rule-breaker is only meaningful against a rule players already know.
- Keep a spreadsheet skeleton: list the target number of components per category, then fill cells over dedicated time blocks. WHY: a visible map turns a daunting set into a finite list of gaps to fill.

## Checklist
- [ ] Top-level categories named and listed.
- [ ] Each category has 3 mechanical answers (does / best at / worst at).
- [ ] Each category has 3 thematic answers (represents / wants / fears).
- [ ] Subcategories defined inside each category where needed.
- [ ] At least one cross-category cycle defined (constant trait + varied trait).
- [ ] At least one intra-category cycle defined (e.g. small/medium/large).
- [ ] Spreadsheet skeleton with target component counts per category.
- [ ] Any intentional rule-breaks documented with the reason they exist.
- [ ] If stuck, fallback chosen: add a cycle or add a subcategory.

## Anti-patterns
- Designing components one by one with no category structure — leads to duplicates, gaps, and inconsistent power.
- Defining categories by theme only, with no mechanical strengths/weaknesses — players cannot reason about them.
- Defining categories by mechanics only, with no theme — components feel interchangeable.
- Breaking structure early, before the structure is established — the exception reads as a mistake, not a highlight.
- Assuming creativity needs no constraints — constraints are what make the creative choices legible.
- Skipping the spreadsheet and holding the component map only in your head — gaps stay invisible.

name: component-development-balance
topic: Game balance during component development
confidence: consensus
sources: ["Think Like A Game Designer, George Rockwell, ch. 15 (pp. 118-122)"]
---
## Rules
- Define balance as "no single strategy is so dominant that another cannot defeat it" — not as all components being equally strong. WHY: equal components remove the joy of discovery; players enjoy finding stronger options.
- Allow some components/strategies to be stronger than others, but cap the top end so the strongest option doesn't invalidate all others. WHY: discovery is fun only while alternatives remain viable.
- Add "silver bullet" components: weak in general, devastating against one specific strategy (e.g., color-specific damage prevention, "Resistance to X" armor). WHY: they keep a dominant strategy answerable without making the bullet itself the best pick.
- Keep silver bullets narrow and weak outside their target matchup. WHY: an overtuned silver bullet becomes the new dominant strategy.
- Count your "development knobs" — each independent balance variable (power number, cost type, resource type, etc.). More knobs = easier balancing. WHY: each knob is a separate axis you can tune without breaking others.
- Design the core engine with multiple knobs from the start if you expect a large component set. WHY: retrofitting balance axes after content exists is far more expensive.
- Treat balance as a range, not a point: verify that each top strategy has at least one viable counter. WHY: rock-paper-scissors style mutual answers prevent any single dominant play.

## Checklist
- [ ] List every distinct strategy a player can pursue with current components.
- [ ] For each strategy, name at least one component or strategy that beats it.
- [ ] Identify the current strongest strategy and confirm it is beatable.
- [ ] Enumerate the balance knobs available in the engine.
- [ ] For each silver bullet, test it in matchups where its target is absent — confirm it's near-useless there.
- [ ] Confirm no silver bullet is strong enough to be a default pick.

## Anti-patterns
- Making all components numerically equal in the name of "balance" — kills discovery and flattens strategy diversity.
- Shipping a silver bullet that is also generically strong — it becomes the dominant strategy it was meant to counter.
- Balancing only by tweaking one knob (e.g., power) when the engine offers several — slower and more brittle.
- Assuming balance can be fully achieved before release — it can't; plan for post-release tuning.

## Rules
- Treat downtime as the #1 engagement killer; fix it before tuning difficulty or adding content. WHY: players who wait have nothing to attend to and disengage.
- In turn-based games, shorten each turn to a single meaningful decision (Poker: raise/call/fold) so opponents stay attentive. WHY: short turns mean less dead time between a player's own actions.
- Give players something to do or watch during opponents' turns (hidden info to read, shared "hot" resource to track). WHY: attention stays on the game even when it is not your turn.
- Prefer real-time or simultaneous-resolution mechanics when the design allows it. WHY: real-time games rarely have downtime because players can always react.
- Cap repetitive actions: if a quest needs 5 kills, do not require 10. WHY: repetition without a sense of progress is grind.
- Break long repetitive stretches with a mid-section change (power-up, new mechanic, new learning opportunity). WHY: novelty restores the feeling of meaningful choice.
- In board games, cut slow phases or accelerate turns when nothing new is happening. WHY: slow phases with no decisions are pure downtime.
- Instrument playtests: if players tune out or stop at the same point, that point is the downtime/grind hotspot. WHY: observed drop-off is the most reliable signal of disengagement.

## Checklist
- [ ] Measure the longest gap between two player decisions; can it be halved?
- [ ] Does every player have a decision or observation available on every turn?
- [ ] Is there a shared resource or hidden-info element that keeps non-active players watching?
- [ ] Count repeated actions in the core loop; is each repetition teaching something new?
- [ ] Identify the slowest phase of a full session and test a shortened version.

## Anti-patterns
- Long turns with multiple sub-decisions for one player while others idle.
- "Kill 10 boars" style quests with no new mechanic introduced along the way.
- Assuming grind is only an online-game problem; it appears in board and single-player games too.
- Adding content to fix engagement when the real problem is waiting time.

## Rules
- Treat difficulty as a two-sided failure: too hard and too easy both cause disengagement. WHY: both remove the tension that holds attention.
- Offer explicit difficulty modes for single-player games (more/less resources, harder/easier challenges). WHY: players opt into the challenge level they want.
- Use dynamic difficulty sparingly and only as an assist (e.g., offer to skip a section after repeated deaths). WHY: some players want explicit control and resent hidden tuning.
- In multiplayer, balance with handicaps rather than symmetric rules: golf handicaps, extra starting pieces for weaker Go players. WHY: a balanced playing field keeps both sides engaged.
- Build matchmaking on a rating system (Elo or a variant) that pairs players of similar skill. WHY: mismatched opponents produce one-sided, disengaging matches.
- Audit matchmaking incentives for perverse effects: if losing costs rating, players may stop playing ranked before big events. WHY: a system that punishes playing discourages play.
- Provide personal objectives and "little wins" so losing players still have goals (personal high score, kill count, deck growth). WHY: personal progress removes the sting of losing the match.
- Design mechanics where each player's own engine improves independently (deckbuilding is the model case). WHY: continuous personal progress keeps trailing players invested.

## Checklist
- [ ] Is there an explicit difficulty option for solo play?
- [ ] If dynamic adjustment exists, is it optional or clearly signposted?
- [ ] Does multiplayer include a handicap or catch-up tool for weaker players?
- [ ] Does matchmaking use a skill rating, and does the rating system reward rather than punish playing?
- [ ] Does every player have at least one personal stat or mini-goal to chase when losing?

## Anti-patterns
- One global difficulty with no opt-in for solo players.
- Hidden dynamic tuning that players cannot see or disable.
- Rating systems where protecting your rank means not playing.
- Winner-takes-all scoring with no personal objectives for the losing side.

## Rules
- Guarantee that players still in the game retain a real chance to win. WHY: forced play with a known loss is one of the most frustrating states.
- Scale comeback mechanics with game length: the longer the game, the more you need them. WHY: long games give leaders more time to lock in an unassailable lead.
- Weight late-game decisions more heavily (e.g., more points in later rounds) to let early deficits be overcome. WHY: it keeps the outcome open until the end.
- Balance comeback strength against early-game impact; treat each phase (beginning/middle/end) as its own mini-game whose prize is an advantage in the next phase. WHY: if early play feels meaningless, players disengage from the start.
- Add small probabilities of extreme outcomes (a lucky streak for the last-place player). WHY: even tiny chances of a dramatic swing raise excitement.
- Make endgame timing uncertain (e.g., game ends after N drawn end-trigger tiles). WHY: if you never know when the game ends, you never know if you still have time to catch up.
- Hide final-score information (hidden victory-point cards, points buried in decks). WHY: obfuscated scores prevent early resignation and keep tension.
- Use negative feedback loops against the leader: better power-ups for last place, leader-targeting items, increased upkeep for the leader, first pick for last place. WHY: they directly counter the leader's advantage.
- Allow player self-regulation in multiplayer: let players choose whom to trade with or attack. WHY: the table can police a runaway leader without designer intervention.

## Checklist
- [ ] Can a player in last place still win with good play plus some luck?
- [ ] Are late-game decisions worth more than early ones, without making early play pointless?
- [ ] Is the game's end time uncertain?
- [ ] Is the final score at least partially hidden?
- [ ] Is there at least one explicit anti-leader mechanic?
- [ ] If self-regulation exists, is politicking an acceptable part of the experience?

## Anti-patterns
- Deterministic scoring visible to all, letting players compute the winner before the end.
- Fixed, known game length with no catch-up tools in a long game.
- Comeback mechanics so strong that early play stops mattering.
- Anti-leader mechanics that feel punitive and turn off competitive players.
- Self-regulation without any counterweight, turning play into politicking and whining.

## Rules
- Apply the cardinal test to every monetization decision: "If I were the target audience, would I be thrilled to play and pay for this game?" WHY: it converts an abstract ethics question into a concrete, checkable player-experience question.
- Require that joy and value delivered clearly outweigh the cost. If you have any doubt, keep iterating until the value is unambiguous. WHY: a marginal value-to-price ratio is where resentment and churn start.
- Set the primary design goal as delivering maximum value to players, with payment structured to support the work and feel fulfilling to the payer. WHY: extraction-first design is both unethical and bad business — it burns the audience you need for the next game.
- Reject "anything goes" reasoning: the fact that monetization isn't evil does not make all monetization schemes acceptable. WHY: there are clearly ethical and unethical ways to charge, and the designer chooses which one.
## Checklist
- [ ] For each paid element, answer the cardinal test in writing.
- [ ] Identify the target audience precisely enough that you can honestly role-play as one of them.
- [ ] Verify the value-to-cost ratio is obviously favorable, not merely defensible.
- [ ] Confirm the payment structure supports continued work on the game.
## Anti-patterns
- Designing primarily to extract as much money as possible from players.
- Shipping monetization you personally would not pay for as a player in that genre.
- Justifying a mechanic because "it's legal" or "competitors do it".
